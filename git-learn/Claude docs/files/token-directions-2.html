Token · design directions · round two

Cleaner, warmer, and nothing that looks like a machine.

The last set leaned technical — monospace codes, industrial grotesques, dense borders. That's what read as robotic. None of these use a monospace font anywhere. Token codes are set in the same everyday sans as the rest of the app, spaced out and using tabular figures so they still scan as codes. Type is larger, spacing is generous, and there are far fewer lines on screen.

Fonts here are ordinary on purpose — Inter, DM Sans, Plus Jakarta, Figtree. Familiar, not exotic.
01

Daylight

Feels like — Apple Wallet, Google Wallet, Wise

White, open and completely calm. One deep green does all the work, cards are large and softly rounded, and there is almost nothing on screen that isn't content. The safest choice and the easiest to love — this is what most people mean when they say an app looks clean.

Midnight

Feels like — CRED, Revolut, Apple Card

Deep charcoal with plenty of empty space, and the token rendered as a beautiful object rather than a list item — a rich jade card with soft depth and large type. This is the direction that feels expensive. Dark also happens to read as secure to most people without the app having to say so.

Porcelain

Feels like — Monzo, N26, Airbnb

The softest of the five. A cool near-white, cards that float on gentle shadows with no borders at all, and a muted slate blue. Reassuring rather than serious — this is the version your mother would find easy, and it makes the app feel like a calm place rather than a control panel.

Evening

Feels like — a private bank, Amex Platinum, Aesop

Warm charcoal with champagne, and a quiet serif used only for headings. That single humanist touch is what stops an app feeling mechanical — everything else stays a plain, familiar sans. Understated luxury rather than technology: it suggests discretion, which is exactly what the product sells.

Feels like — Notion, Linear, Cash App

Almost entirely black and white, with one confident blue that appears only where you can act. Big type, wide spacing, no ornament at all. Modern and young without being loud, and by far the easiest of the five to build consistently — every screen writes itself once the rule is set.

Where I'd put my money

Daylight if you want the widest possible appeal — nobody has ever disliked it, it photographs beautifully for the store listing, and it will still look right in five years. Midnight if you want people to feel something when they open it; in India especially, dark and expensive-looking is what "premium app" means to most people right now. Porcelain if the audience skews older or less technical.

Evening and Signal are both good, but for different reasons: Evening is the most distinctive and would give you a real brand, while Signal is the most reliable to execute alone because the rules are so simple you can't get it wrong.

Three things that would help me get this right first time
  • Name two or three apps on your own phone that you think look premium. That single answer tells me more than any brief — I'll match the qualities rather than guess at them.
  • Light or dark as the main experience? Both get built eventually, but one has to be the face of the product and they lead to quite different layouts.
  • Who do you picture opening this? A 26-year-old selling a phone on OLX and a 45-year-old avoiding broker calls want the app to look like two different things.
