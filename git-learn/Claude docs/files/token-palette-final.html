Token · recommended palette

The interface has no colour. Your doors do.

Token's resting state is nothing happening — nobody can reach you, and that's the product. So the interface should be quiet by default, and colour should exist for exactly one reason: to report the state of a door. That single rule settles every decision below, and it means the palette gets more useful as you accumulate tokens rather than noisier.

Black is the brand. Colour is information. Nothing decorative is ever coloured.

Four colours, four meanings

Each is deep and slightly desaturated rather than bright. Saturated colour is what makes an app tiring at the twentieth open of the day; deep colour reads as considered and stays comfortable at night. Every one of these survives translation — green, amber and red are the traffic light, which is the closest thing the world has to a universal visual language.

#1D6F52
Evergreen — open
A door is live and someone can walk through it. Green is the global grammar of permission, and permission is literally what a token grants.
#A8711A
Ochre — closing
Time is running out. Warm enough to notice on a glance, calm enough that it never reads as an emergency.
#4E5A6B
Slate — paused
Held, not gone. A distinct colour matters here: paused is reversible and revoked isn't, and the interface must never blur those two.
#B04238
Brick — revoke
Appears on exactly one action in the entire app. Because it's rationed, it carries real weight when you finally see it.
#131316
Ink — the brand
Buttons, active tabs, your own messages. Warm-tinted rather than pure black, which stops the app feeling clinical.
#F7F7F5
Canvas — near-neutral
A whisper of warmth, well short of cream. Cold greys read as clinical and tiring; this reads as calm without becoming a mood.

The same screen, day and night

A messaging app is used in bed. Dark mode isn't a preference here, it's half the usage — so the palette is designed to invert rather than being a light theme with the lights turned off. The state colours brighten slightly in the dark so they stay legible without glowing.

Why this, and not the alternatives

Every version I showed you before had the same hidden flaw: the brand colour and the state colour were the same colour. When violet is the brand, violet is on buttons, tabs, badges and links — so violet can no longer mean "this door is open." The signal drowns in the branding. Making black the brand frees green to do actual work.

The other change is saturation. Bright accents photograph well and feel cheap by month three. Deep, slightly muted colour is what expensive, long-lived software looks like — and it's the only kind that's comfortable at 11pm, which is when a lot of messaging happens.

On typography
One family, Inter, nothing else
No monospace anywhere — that's what read as robotic. Token codes use Inter with tabular figures and wide letter-spacing, so they scan as codes while staying human. One family is also the single easiest way to look intentional when you're building solo.
On the signature
The life bar is the memorable thing
If colour is rationed, identity has to come from structure. Every token carries a thin line draining toward zero — the one device unique to this product, and it's functional rather than decorative.
On going global
Nothing here needs translation
Green-amber-red is the traffic light; it works in Mumbai, Lagos and Berlin without explanation. No culture-specific meaning, no colour that changes register across markets.
Where I might be wrong
This is quieter than most Indian consumer apps
CRED and Jupiter won on richness, not restraint, and some users read quiet as unfinished. If it feels too plain once you're using it, the fix is more green — not a second brand colour.

This is what I'd build. It comes from the product rather than from a reference, it holds up at four tokens and at forty, it works at midnight, and every colour on screen earns its place. Say the word and the full prototype follows in it — v7 skeleton, every decision we've made, light and dark.
