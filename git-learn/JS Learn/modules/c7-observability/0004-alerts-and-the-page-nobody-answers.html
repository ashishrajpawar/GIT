Alerts, and the Page Nobody Answers

C7 — Lesson 4 of 4  |  ~50 min

Why this matters for Token

Three lessons have produced logs, crash reports and metrics. None of them wakes anybody up, and that is the point of this one — along with the reason it is the last: you cannot alert on a number you are not collecting.

The company is two people. The same arithmetic that forced ADR-0011's automatic suspension applies here: there is no rota that turns two into three, and a pager rotation of two is one person being woken by every alert half the time. So the alert set has to be small enough that every page is real, and everything else has to be something that waits until morning without anybody feeling nervous about it.

1 · The rule this course keeps arriving at

C2/0001 said it about a build that is always red. C7/0002 said it about a tracker full of expected failures. Here it is again, at the layer where it costs the most:

An alert that fires and is ignored is worse than no alert, because it occupies the space where a working one would go.

The mechanism is always the same. Something fires often and means nothing; people learn to dismiss it; the dismissal becomes a reflex applied to the whole channel; and then the real one arrives and is dismissed in the same second by somebody who is not being careless, just fast. Nobody decides to ignore alerts. It is trained.

2 · Alert on what the user feels

The distinction that removes most bad alerts is symptom versus cause.

A cause alert is a guess about which resource will run out first. Sometimes the guess is wrong, and then the alert is loud when nothing is wrong and silent when everything is. A symptom alert — error rate, latency, traffic disappearing — is about what somebody is actually experiencing, and it is true regardless of which component caused it.

Causes still belong on a dashboard. That is where you look after the symptom alert fires, and it is exactly what the runbook tells you to open.

3 · The alert that cannot fire when the service is down

Here is the trap the previous lesson was built to set up. An error-rate alert is errors / count > 0.05, and if the service has stopped receiving requests entirely, count is zero.

So null is a third answer, and it must never be read as “fine”

An alert rule evaluating a missing value has three possible outcomes, not two: over, under, and no data. Collapsing the third into “under” is the failure above, and collapsing it into “over” pages you every time an exporter restarts.

The decision taken here: missing data raises a ticket, never a page — because a metric that has stopped arriving is far more often the exporter than the service, and a page needs a human who can act right now. The cost, stated rather than hidden: when the service genuinely is down, this rule is not what tells you. That job belongs to a separate alert on traffic falling to zero, which is why the previous lesson insisted that count and errorRate stay separate fields rather than one summary number.

4 · Sustained, not instantaneous

One scrape over the line is noise: a slow query, a garbage collection pause, a deploy. An alert fires only when the condition has held for some duration, and the timer works in both directions — the moment the value drops back under the threshold, the clock is cleared.

That clearing is the part people get wrong twice over:

  • Forgetting to clear it means a condition that was briefly over an hour ago combines with one that is briefly over now, and the alert fires for a problem that never lasted a minute.
  • Resetting it on every evaluation — writing now into the pending timestamp each time round — means the elapsed time is always zero and the alert can never fire at all. This one is worse, because it looks like everything is fine.

5 · Page or ticket, and the question that decides it

PageTicket
Wakes somebodyYesNo
TestA human is needed now, and there is something they can doEverything else
Token examplesRedemption failing; the API down; TLS expiring todayBackup older than expected; disk 70% full; a metric stopped arriving

Both halves of the test are required. “A human is needed now” and “there is something they can do” are different questions, and an alert that passes only the first is the definition of a page that trains people to ignore pages.

An alert with no runbook cannot be a page

A runbook is four things: what this alert means, what to open first, what to do, and how to know it is over. Without one, a page at 3am is a puzzle handed to somebody at their least capable, and the honest response is to make it a ticket instead.

This is the same shape as the forced update with no store link in C2/0004. In both cases the configuration is what is missing, and in both cases the person who did not write it is not the person who should pay for it. Downgrading is reversible and visible; waking somebody up is neither.
