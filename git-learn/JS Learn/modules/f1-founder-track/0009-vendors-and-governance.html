Vendors & Infrastructure Governance

F1 — Lesson 9 of 16  |  a DPA with each processor is a legal requirement, not paperwork

Why this matters for Token

Under DPDP, anyone who processes personal data on your instructions is a Data Processor, and you are required to have a contract with them covering that processing. That contract is the data processing agreement. It is not optional and it is not satisfied by having clicked accept on a signup page — though for most cloud vendors the DPA is a document they publish and you accept, so it is usually a matter of finding and filing it rather than negotiating one.

The reason this lesson is worth real attention is different. Every vendor is a place where your privacy promise can be broken by someone who never read it. You told users the server holds no key and that backups roll in seven days. A vendor that keeps object versions forever, or an error tracker that attaches request bodies by default, quietly makes both of those sentences false.

1 · The vendor inventory

Start from the RoPA in lesson 0001. Every row you marked “processed by a vendor” produces an entry here. Work by role, not by brand name — roles survive an architecture change and brand names do not.

The source task board named LiveKit and AWS Mumbai in this list. That is superseded: call relay is self-hosted coturn, hosting is a Coolify VPS, and third-party communications SDKs stay out of scope entirely. The task board’s vendor line is the stale document, not this one.

What this buys you: the relay is not a processor, so there is no DPA for it, no residency answer for call media, and nothing to declare on either store form about media passing through a third party. For a product whose promise is that neither party learns the other’s network address, keeping the relay in-house is the version of that promise with the fewest people in it.

Cost of the option rejected: a managed relay would have been less to operate and would have moved a capacity problem onto someone else’s bill. It would also have added a processor with media passing through it, a DPA, a residency answer, a “shared with third parties” entry on both store forms, and a direct contradiction of the no-third-party-comms-SDK rule.

The two costs you now own instead, and both are real:

  • Relay bandwidth is a line item, and a large one. The relay is not a vendor, but it is not free either: every call is relayed rather than a fraction, and each one crosses the server twice — in from one party, out to the other. Size the bill on 100% of calls doubled. Roughly 900 MB per hour of 480p video means a 1 TB allowance is about 2,200 hours — reachable with around seventy users doing an hour a day — and under 750 hours at 720p.
  • A coturn outage is a total call outage, not a degraded one. Relay-only means there is no direct path to fall back to. That is the accepted price of not handing either party the other’s address, and it means coturn needs monitoring that pages a human, same as the SMS aggregator.

2 · The data processing agreement 🔴 ⚖️

Obtain one from every vendor in the inventory that touches personal data. For most cloud vendors this is a published document you accept and file; for a smaller Indian SMS aggregator it may need asking for, and asking is normal.

What to check in each one:

ClauseWhat good looks likeThe trap
Scope of processingNames the data, the purpose and the durationA generic clause covering “any data” tells you nothing and protects nobody
Instructions onlyThey process on your instructions and not for their own purposesA carve-out permitting use for “service improvement” — that is their purpose, not yours
Sub-processorsDisclosed, with notice before changesA right to add sub-processors silently. Your vendor’s vendor is also handling your users’ data
Security measuresSpecific, not “industry standard”An unfalsifiable phrase in place of a commitment
Breach notificationThey tell you fast enough for you to meet your clocks“Without undue delay” is not a number. Your CERT-In clock is measured in hours; a vendor taking three days makes you late
Deletion and returnData returned or destroyed at the end, on request, with confirmationNo obligation to delete, or an unlimited retention for “backup purposes”
AuditSome right to evidence — a report, a certification, an answered questionnaireNothing at all
Liability capProportionate to the harm, not to the monthly feeLiability capped at one month of fees. Common, rarely negotiable, and worth knowing even when you cannot change it

You will not negotiate most of these as a small company. That is fine. The point of reading them is to know which risks you have accepted, so that they can be managed elsewhere — a vendor with a weak breach-notification clause is a vendor whose failures you need your own monitoring to detect.

3 · The two vendors that break your promises by default

These deserve their own section because in both cases the default configuration is the dangerous one, and nothing errors.

Error tracking attaches request bodies by default

Every serious error tracker will, out of the box, attach the HTTP request that caused the error — headers, and often the body.

For Token that is precisely the wrong body. The token code was deliberately moved out of the URL and into the request body, so that it would not reach the proxy access log, browser history, or a referrer header. That decision routes it straight into the error tracker instead, where it is stored by a third party, indexed, and visible to anyone with access to your error dashboard.

Two fixes, both needed:

  • Configure the tracker’s scrubbing to drop request bodies, or to allow-list the fields you want.
  • Log an allow-list, never a copy-with-deletions. Removing the one dangerous field you thought of is not the same as choosing the fields you meant to keep, and the difference shows up the day someone adds a field.

The general rule for the logs, worth repeating to whoever writes them: log the id. An id identifies the row for anyone who can already query the database, and is worth nothing to anyone who cannot. That is exactly the property an identifier in a log should have. A code is a capability and does not have it.

Off-site backup copies never delete, and that is why they feel safe

Your published sentence is that copies in backups are overwritten within seven days. That is a claim about every copy.

The commands normally used to push a backup off-site — a plain copy to a remote, a plain archive sync to a second machine — do not remove anything at the destination. They are chosen precisely because they cannot destroy the far end. That property is what makes your sentence false, and neither command will ever complain.

So ask the vendor-side questions explicitly:

  • Does the bucket have versioning on? Versions are copies. A deleted object with versioning on is not deleted.
  • Are there lifecycle rules, and do they match your published number?
  • Does the hosting control panel take its own backups you did not configure? Many do, and they are on their own schedule.
  • Is there a soft-delete or trash period, and how long?

Every location a dump reaches needs the same clock. Count the locations; do not assume there are two.

Push notifications — the payload is your decision

Push is the one unavoidable third party, because only Google and Apple can wake a backgrounded app. What reaches them is entirely up to you.

A notification can never contain a message. Not as policy — as fact. The server holds ciphertext and no key, so there is no preview for it to put there. If a notification payload has ever had a preview field in it, that field could only ever have been filled by something that should not have had access.

What that means for the vendor relationship: what Google and Apple receive is a wake-up and a generic title. Not the sender, not the content, not the token code. A leaked code is a capability and revoking the token takes it back; a leaked message body is the thing the entire architecture exists to protect.

4 · Data residency

  • Confirm where each vendor stores the data, in writing, per service. A vendor with an Indian region does not automatically use it for every service you buy from them — logs, backups and support tooling often live somewhere else by default.
  • Primary storage in India is the sensible default for an Indian consumer product, both for latency and for the regulatory conversation.
  • Write down where backups go as a separate answer from where the database lives. It is a different location and it is frequently a different country.
  • Note transfers in the privacy policy if any personal data leaves India, and be specific about which data and to whom.

6 · What happens if a vendor fails

Write a one-paragraph answer per vendor. Not a disaster recovery plan — just the shape of the response, decided while calm.

Vendor failsWhat breaksPlan
SMS aggregatorNobody can sign in. New devices are locked out entirelyDecided: a second aggregator is pre-registered on DLT. Registration takes weeks and cannot be done during an outage — lesson 0012
Call relay (your own coturn)All calls fail, not some. Relay-only means there is no direct path to fall back toAccepted cost of the privacy design, and now yours to operate. Monitor it as a critical service; messaging keeps working
Hosting providerEverythingThe reason backups are off-site and the deployment is containers. Know your restore time, and test it
Push providerNo wake-ups. Messages still arrive when the app opensDegraded, not down. Make sure the app fetches on foreground
Error trackingYou are blind to new bugsAnnoying only. Do not let it block a deploy
Object storageAvatars break; backups stop leaving the serverThe second failure is the serious one, and it is silent. Alert on backup upload failure

Two of those rows are worth internalising: the SMS aggregator is a single point of total failure for sign-in, and the call relay is a single point of total failure for calls — the second one deliberately, as the price of not exposing either party’s network address. Neither has a graceful degradation. Both need monitoring that pages a human.

As of 2026-08-27 they are handled differently, on purpose. The SMS one has a redundancy answer — a second aggregator, registered in advance — because a competing supplier exists and registering one takes weeks you will not have. The relay has a recovery answer only: you run it, so restoring it is your job and there is nobody to fail over to. That asymmetry is worth knowing before an outage rather than during one.

What this costs you

  • A day of collecting and filing documents, plus follow-up with any vendor who does not publish a DPA.
  • Terms you cannot negotiate and have to accept. Small-company reality. The value is in knowing which ones.
  • A second SMS aggregator registered on DLT before you need it — real setup effort, weeks of lead time, for something you hope never to use. It is the difference between an outage of hours and an outage of weeks.
  • Total call failure when the relay is down, by design. That is the price of not handing either party the other’s network address, and it is the right trade for this product — but it must be a known one, not a discovery.
