Positioning & Go-to-Market

F1 — Lesson 7 of 16  |  upstream of every word in both store listings

Start from the failure, not from the pitch

A third-party review of this product concluded that Token was a queue-management app. Someone read the material, formed a confident and completely wrong model of what it does, and moved on.

That is not a small marketing problem. It is the same failure mode as a store reviewer who cannot find the reporting button: the product is judged on what it appears to be within a few seconds, and no amount of correct detail further down the page repairs a wrong first model. Everything in this lesson is downstream of fixing it.

1 · Diagnose the comprehension problem

Read the review that got it wrong. Then work out why, because there are at least three independent causes and fixing one does not fix the others.

CauseEvidenceFixable by
The nameIn India, a token is the numbered slip you take at a bank or a hospital. That meaning is common, concrete, and arrives before yoursLesson 0003 — and possibly not fixable without a rename
The first screenIt shows a list. A list of what, to a person who does not yet know what a token is, looks like a queueA rewrite of the empty state and the first-run screen
The absent premiseNowhere does anything say what problem this solves before showing the mechanism. The app explains how before it has said whyOne sentence, placed first, everywhere
The third cause is the real one

Most products explain their mechanism and assume the problem is obvious. For Token the problem genuinely is not obvious, because the cost of handing out your phone number is diffuse and delayed — the spam call arrives months later and you never connect it to the property listing you enquired about.

So the premise has to be supplied, not assumed. And it has to be supplied in the form of a moment the reader recognises, not a category. “Privacy” is a category. “You enquired about a flat once and they still call you” is a moment.

Propose three fixes and test them

Do not pick one and roll it out. Write three candidate first-screens, show each to different people, and count who explains it back correctly. Three worth trying:

  1. The problem first. A form asking for a phone number, and a line: “Every time you type your number into a form, you lose control of it. Token gives you something else to type.”
  2. The mechanic first, in one animation. A code being issued, used, and then revoked, with the page going dead. No text but the labels.
  3. The comparison. Two columns: what they get today (your number, forever, shared with anyone) versus what they get with Token (a code, for two weeks, revocable).

The test is not “which do you prefer?” — people are bad at that. The test is: show it for ten seconds, take it away, and ask “what is this app for?” Count correct answers.

Rewrite the Home screen brief and the onboarding copy

The standard is a hard one and worth stating as a rule: the premise must land in two seconds, before any interaction, for someone who has never heard of the product. If the screen requires a tap to make sense, it has failed.

The empty state is the highest-value screen in the whole app, because it is what every new user sees and what every store reviewer screenshots. An empty token list that says “No tokens yet” has thrown away the single best opportunity the product gets.

2 · The positioning statement

One sentence. Write it, then derive every other piece of copy from it.

THE SHAPE

For <who>, who <problem>, Token is a <category> that
<what it does>. Unlike <the alternative>, Token <the
difference that matters>.


A WORKED DRAFT

For anyone who has to give a phone number to a stranger --
a seller, a landlord, a service, a company -- Token is an app
that gives out a revocable code instead. Unlike a second SIM
or a burner number, the code carries your rules, works for
one relationship, and dies the moment you say so.

Then, from that one sentence:

ArtefactLengthRule
Store short description~80 charactersMust contain the problem, not just the mechanism
One paragraph3–4 sentencesProblem, mechanism, what makes it different, what it is not
Elevator version20 seconds spokenSay it out loud. If you stumble, it is too clever
Who it is forA listNamed segments, ranked — see §3
What it replacesOne lineBe specific. “Giving out your number”
What it is notA listNot a second SIM. Not a messaging app. Not a queue system

Test on ten strangers. Read them the paragraph, then ask them to explain it back. Not “does that make sense?” — everyone says yes. If seven of ten cannot explain it back, the sentence is wrong, not the strangers.

3 · Strategy

🔴 Resolve replacement versus supplement

This is the strategic question underneath everything else, and it has to be settled with the technical founder because it changes what gets built.

ReplacementSupplement
The claimToken is where these conversations happen. You stop giving out your numberToken is for the risky cases. You still give out your number to people you trust
RetentionDaily or weekly use. Habit-formingOccasional, bursty. Months between sessions
PricingSubscription is naturalSubscription is hard. Usage-based or freemium fits better
The recipient sideMust be excellent. It is where most of the actual usage happensMust merely work. It is a one-off interaction
MetricsWeekly active use is meaningfulWeekly active use is a vanity metric that will look terrible

Choosing wrong is not a copywriting error; it means optimising the wrong half of the product for a year. And note the asymmetry: supplement is the safer bet and the harder business. Decide explicitly, write down which, and write down what evidence would change your mind.

Rank the segments

The product is worth most where someone is handing an identifier to an entity that does not know them and will keep it forever. It is worth least with people who already have the number.

SegmentWhy it is strongWhat is hard about it
Marketplace sellingA listing means dozens of strangers with your number, permanently. The pain is immediate and universally recognisedBuyers must be willing to use a web page rather than call
Property — renting or buyingThe classic case. One enquiry, years of broker callsAgents want a number and may refuse a code
Home servicesRepeat strangers at your door with your number and your addressLow frequency per user
Public-facing professionsDoctors, teachers, agents, journalists: contactable by necessity, harassable by consequenceSmaller population, but high willingness to pay
Family circles—The weakest segment. See below
Document why family is the weakest segment

Because they already have your number. The entire mechanism — issue, restrict, revoke — is a response to a stranger holding a permanent identifier, and none of that applies to someone who has had your number for fifteen years and whose calls you want.

Write this down, because it is the segment founders drift toward: it is the easiest to recruit (you know them), the easiest to demo, and it produces feedback that is entirely misleading. A product tested exclusively on family looks unnecessary, because for family it is unnecessary.

The objection-handling sheet

Write the honest answers, including the ones that concede something. A sheet that wins every argument is a sheet nobody believes.

ObjectionHonest answer
“How is this different from a burner number?”A second number is one identifier shared with everyone; when it gets spammed you change it and tell everyone. A token is per-relationship, carries rules, and dies alone. Also: no second SIM, no monthly fee, and it works from a web page
“They will just refuse and demand my number.”Some will. That is real and it is why the request-a-token flow exists — when a business asks first, the friction is on their side. Expect partial adoption at the start
“Why would the other person use a web page?”They install nothing and sign up for nothing. It is a link. Lower friction than an app they would have refused
“What if you get subpoenaed?”We hand over what we have: that an account exists, token metadata, timestamps. We cannot hand over messages because we hold no key. That is a design decision, documented
“What stops abuse if you cannot read messages?”Contact is denied by default — nobody reaches you without a token you issued. Beyond that, the recipient reports, with proof, and we act within 24 hours. Lesson 0006
“I already gave my number to a hundred places.”Token does nothing about those. See below
Never claim retroactive protection

If someone already has your number, Token does nothing. It cannot un-share what is shared; it can only change what you hand over next time.

Say this plainly and early, in the objection sheet, in press answers, and in onboarding. It is the single easiest claim to imply accidentally — “take back control of your number” implies exactly this — and the fastest way to lose a privacy audience is a promise that does not survive their first five seconds of thought.

4 · Launch gates

Three stages, and for each: what must be learned, and what would stop progression. A gate with no stop condition is not a gate.

StageWhat must be learnedStop if
Closed test (12 testers, 14 days)Does the two-party flow work on real devices? Do people understand what the app is without you explaining it?Testers cannot complete a redemption unaided; or fewer than half can say what the app is for
Open testDoes the recipient side survive strangers? What breaks at small scale? Do people come back?Reports arriving faster than you can triage; a privacy defect of any size; recipients abandoning the page
ProductionDoes anyone activate — issue a token and have it redeemed by someone real?Store compliance incomplete; the 24-hour SLA unstaffed; the deletion page not live

Plan the sequence and the timing backwards from the two hard dependencies: DLT registration before the closed test can start, and fourteen continuous days before production access. Lessons 0012 and 0004.

5 · Metrics

Activation is the first token redeemed, not the install

An install means somebody was curious. A token issued means they tried. A token redeemed by another person means the product did the thing it exists to do — and it is the first point at which the user has experienced the value rather than the setup.

Choosing the redemption as the activation event also has a useful side effect: it forces you to care about the recipient experience, which is the half of the product with no app, no onboarding, and no second chance.

Retention, for a low-frequency product

If Token is a supplement, weekly active users will look dreadful and will mean nothing. Someone who issues one token when they list a sofa, and nothing for four months, is a successful user. Define retention as something that survives that:

  • Return-to-issue: of users who issued a token, how many issue another within 90 days? That is the real repeat signal.
  • Live token coverage: does the user still have at least one working token out there? A user with three live tokens is retained even in a silent month.
  • Revocation as a health signal, not churn: someone revoking a token is the product working, not a user leaving.

Choose five and reject the rest

MetricWhy this one
Activation rateInstalls that reach a first redemption. The one number that says the product works
Return-to-issue at 90 daysRepeat use, measured on the product’s real rhythm
Holder completion rateRedemption pages opened that lead to a message sent. Measures the half you cannot see
Median time to first response on reportsThe SLA you promised two stores
Comprehension rateNew users who issue a token within their first session. Your ongoing test of whether the premise lands

Everything else — daily actives, screen views, session length, time in app — reject explicitly and in writing. For this product, time in app going down is usually good.

Analytics that do not compromise the claim

You are a privacy product. Your analytics are part of the product claim, and the wrong choice here is one screenshot away from being the story about you.

  • Self-hosted or nothing. A third-party analytics SDK in a privacy app is an unforced error, and it lands you in the “data shared with third parties” box on both store forms.
  • Count events, not people. You need “how many redemptions completed this week”, not a per-user timeline.
  • Never anything about message content, token codes, or holder identity. The codes are capabilities; the content is what the whole architecture exists to protect.
  • Whatever you collect appears on the store forms and in the RoPA. If adding a metric means editing lesson 0001’s table, it had better be one of the five.

6 · Outreach

  • Build the journalist list — people covering privacy, consumer tech and Indian digital policy, by name, with what they last wrote. Twenty relevant names beat a purchased list of five hundred.
  • Write the pitch — three sentences: the problem in a form they recognise, what you built, why it is different. The DPDP angle is genuinely newsworthy for this product: a consent-and-revocation app in the year the consent law lands.
  • Identify communities without spamming them. Read the rules, participate first, and disclose that it is yours. One post in a community that tolerates it is worth more than ten that get removed and remembered.
  • Prepare the founder story — what made you build this. It should be true and specific.
  • Prepare the hardest questions in advance: how is this different from a burner number, what stops abuse, what if you get subpoenaed. All three are in the objection sheet above. Rehearse them out loud; these are the questions that decide whether the piece is favourable.

What this costs you

  • A possible rename, if the comprehension work concludes the name is the main cause. That decision belongs to lesson 0003 and this lesson supplies its evidence.
  • Metrics that look bad on purpose. Rejecting daily actives means your dashboard will never show the number investors ask for first. You will need the replacement argument ready.
  • Self-hosted analytics — more work than dropping in an SDK, and the only option consistent with the product.
  • Ten strangers, repeatedly. The test is uncomfortable and it is the only thing that tells you whether any of this worked.
