Operational Security & Brand Defence

F1 — Lesson 15 of 16  |  protecting the company’s own accounts, and the brand users check against

Why this matters for Token

You have spent this entire module protecting users’ data. This lesson protects the things that would make all of it irrelevant in an afternoon.

Losing the Play Console account is losing the app. Not the code — the listing, the reviews, the install base, the ability to ship an update. Losing the domain is worse, because a product whose recipient experience is a URL cannot survive somebody else controlling that URL.

And there is one attack that is specific to this design and entirely predictable: a cloned redemption page. A holder clicking a link they were given has no account, no app and no relationship with you. The only thing they can check is the address bar.

1 · Founder account security

🔴 2FA on everything

AccountLosing it means
Play ConsoleThe Android app is gone. Reviews, installs, updates, all of it
App Store ConnectSame for iOS
Hosting / VPS providerThe servers and the database
Domain registrarThe identity of the product. See below
DNS providerSilent redirection of your own users to somebody else
Company emailPassword resets for everything above
Source code hostingThe code, and the ability to push a malicious update
SMS aggregatorSign-in, and the ability to send messages that appear to be from you
Payment gateway, when it existsMoney

Use app-based or hardware second factors. Avoid SMS-based 2FA on these accounts specifically — it is the weakest form, and you of all people should understand why: your own product’s threat model already accepts that whoever controls the SIM controls the account. Do not make your company’s infrastructure depend on that too.

The shared credential vault

  • One vault, both founders, with per-item sharing. Not a spreadsheet, not a chat thread, not one person’s browser.
  • Recovery contacts and backup codes for every critical account, stored in the vault — and the vault’s own recovery stored outside it, somewhere physical. A vault whose recovery key is inside the vault is a locked room with the key inside.
  • Note which account each credential belongs to and which email it recovers to. Recovery chains are how attackers move sideways, and they are how you get locked out.

The bus factor

Write down what happens if one founder is unreachable for two weeks. Not hypothetically — illness, travel, a lost phone.

For each critical account:
  Who has access today?
  If only one person does, why, and by when will that change?
  What is the recovery path if that person is unreachable?
  Where is it written down, and can the other person find it?

For each duty on the compliance calendar:
  Who covers it? -- the 24-hour report SLA especially

For the vault itself:
  Can the other founder get in if the first is unreachable?
  Test this. Do not assume it.

A two-person company has a bus factor of one for most things by default. The goal is not to eliminate that; it is to know exactly where it applies and to have written the recovery down.

Domain and email hardening

  • Lock the domain against transfer. Registrar lock as a minimum; registry lock if offered.
  • Auto-renew on, on a card that outlives the registration. An expired domain is a takeover waiting for someone to notice.
  • SPF, DKIM and DMARC configured so that mail claiming to be from your domain can be verified. Without them anyone can send convincing mail as you — and for a product whose users are being told to watch for impersonation, an unprotected domain is an open door. Move DMARC to a rejecting policy once you have watched the reports for a while.
  • Registrar and DNS on separate accounts from everything else, ideally with different recovery email. They are the two that make every other control irrelevant.

2 · Phishing and impersonation

The cloned redemption page is the attack on this product

Consider what a holder actually experiences. They are given a code by someone they may barely know. They open a link, on a domain they have never seen, and type their name into it. There is no app, no account, no prior relationship and nothing familiar.

That is an ideal phishing target, and the payoff is real: a convincing clone harvests whatever a holder types, and if the clone also forwards the code to the real page it can burn a single-use token or open the conversation itself.

Three defences, all needed — and with the name staying unregistered (lesson 0003), these are not belt-and-braces. They are the whole belt.

  • Own the lookalike domains before someone else does — the misspellings, the hyphen, the alternate TLDs.
  • 🔁 Monitor for clones. Certificate transparency logs show new certificates issued for names resembling yours. Watch them; it is cheap and automatable.
  • Teach users what a genuine link looks like in the help content, in one sentence with the exact domain written out. This is the only defence that works on the clone you have not found yet.
Decided 2026-08-27 — the five most convincing lookalikes

Register five, not one and not twelve. Pick them by asking a single question of each candidate: would a holder glance at this in an address bar and accept it? That question, not a list of TLD suffixes, is what selects the five.

In practice that is the letter-swap or doubled-letter misspelling, the hyphenated form, the singular/plural pair, and the one or two alternate endings a phisher would actually reach for in this market.

Cost: a small annual bill, forever, on the compliance calendar with the other renewals — and it denies the addresses a phisher would pick first, not every address they could invent.

Cost of the options rejected: the primary domain alone is cheapest and means someone else registers the obvious misspelling before you do, leaving you with a takedown and no registration behind it. A dozen-plus portfolio is a meaningful recurring bill for a pre-revenue company and still cannot cover everything, because the space of convincing variants is not finite.

Point them at the real site. A defensive domain parked on a registrar holding page is a wasted asset and looks abandoned. Redirect each one to your primary domain, so a user who mistypes lands where they meant to go — which converts a defensive purchase into a small usability win.

What would reverse it: an actual clone appearing on a variant you did not buy. That is evidence about which five were the right five, and it is worth re-picking the list rather than simply adding a sixth.

The takedown process

1. EVIDENCE FIRST
   Screenshot the page. Record the exact URL, the date, the
   IP it resolves to, and who registered the domain if that
   is public. You will need this for every route below and
   the page may vanish.

2. THE ROUTES, in parallel -- not in sequence
   Registrar abuse contact   the domain itself
   Hosting provider abuse    the server it sits on
   Google Safe Browsing      gets it flagged in Chrome and
                             Android, which is where most of
                             your users are
   Certificate authority     if a certificate was mis-issued
   App store reports         for an impersonating app

3. WHAT TO SEND
   The URL, the evidence, your trademark position if you
   have one (lesson 0003), and a plain statement of harm:
   this page impersonates our service and collects data
   from our users. Be factual. Abuse desks process volume

4. TELL YOUR USERS
   A note in help content and in the app if it is
   widespread. Name the real domain again

5. LOG IT
   Every clone, every takedown, how long it took. This is
   also transparency-report material
  • Watch the app stores for impersonating apps. Search your own name monthly. An app claiming to be Token that asks for a phone number is the mobile version of the same attack.
  • Watch social platforms for accounts using your name, especially any offering “support”.

What to teach users about verifying a link

Every genuine Token link starts with:

    https://<your-exact-domain>/t/

Nothing else. Not a similar spelling. Not a different
ending. If the address bar says anything else, close it and
write to support@<domain>.

We will never ask you for your recovery phrase or a
one-time code. Not by message, not by email, not on the
phone. Anyone who does is not us.

That second paragraph must match what your support team actually does — the rule from lesson 0008. A single support agent asking for a code makes the warning worthless and every phishing attempt plausible.

3 · Responsible disclosure

Security researchers will look at Token, because a privacy product with a public web page is an interesting target. Whether that is helpful or damaging depends almost entirely on whether they had somewhere to send a finding.

PieceWhat it contains
A vulnerability disclosure policyHow to report, what is in scope, what is out, what you commit to — usually acknowledgement within a few days and a fix timeline
A safe-harbour statementThat you will not pursue researchers acting in good faith within the policy. Without this, careful people stay silent
A security.txt file on the domainThe machine-readable version. It is where researchers look first
A named triager and a response timeOne person, one commitment
Acknowledgement or rewardEven a hall-of-fame page. For a privacy brand this is credibility, not charity
security.txt  -- served at /.well-known/security.txt

Contact: mailto:security@<domain>
Expires: <a date, and keep it current>
Preferred-Languages: en, hi
Policy: https://<domain>/security
Acknowledgments: https://<domain>/security/thanks

Set the scope explicitly, and think about the holder side when you do: the redemption page, the API, the mobile app. Out of scope: anything requiring physical access, social engineering of your staff, denial of service, and reports from automated scanners with no demonstrated impact.

A disclosure report may also be an incident

If a researcher demonstrates that user data was accessible, that is not only a bug report. It may start the clocks in lessons 0001 and 0012 — the six-hour one in particular.

So the triage step is: fix it, and separately, decide whether this is reportable. Those are two different questions and the second one has a clock on it.

4 · Beta testers

Your twelve Play testers — lesson 0004 — will see an unfinished product, possibly with real bugs affecting real privacy. Handle it lightly but handle it.

TESTER AGREEMENT -- one page

You are testing an unreleased version of Token.

  1. Please do not share screenshots or details publicly
     until we launch.
  2. Please tell us about anything broken, including
     anything that looks like a privacy problem. We would
     much rather hear it from you.
  3. We may use your feedback to improve the product. We
     will not quote you by name without asking.
  4. This is a test build. Do not use it for anything you
     would be upset to lose.
  5. You can stop at any time -- just tell us, so we can
     keep our tester count correct.

Name                        Date
Email used for testing

Collect these before distributing builds, not after. Point 5 is doing real work: the fourteen-day count breaks if someone silently drops out, and asking them to tell you makes that far less likely.

What this costs you

  • An afternoon of account hygiene, and the ongoing friction of hardware keys and a vault. Cheap against losing a store account.
  • Defensive domain registrations, annually, forever.
  • A monitoring duty that will produce false alarms. Certificate transparency will surface names that resemble yours and are innocent. Triage them anyway; the one that is not innocent looks identical.
  • A public commitment to researchers that you then have to honour, including on a week when you are busy. The alternative is a researcher who publishes instead of writing.
  • Accepting that you cannot fully solve the clone problem. A URL typed by a stranger on an unfamiliar domain is structurally phishable, and the mitigation is user education plus fast takedowns rather than prevention.
