Age, Consumers & Fair Dealing

F1 — Lesson 13 of 16  |  one decision here shapes the whole signup flow

Why this matters for Token

Three separate regimes converge on the same few screens: DPDP’s rules about children, consumer protection law about refunds and cancellation, and the dark-pattern guidelines about how choices are presented. All three are about the same thing — whether a person was genuinely free to choose.

For a product whose whole premise is giving people control over who can reach them, being caught using a manipulative cancellation flow would be worse than an ordinary compliance failure. It would be evidence that the premise was marketing.

1 · Children under DPDP

🔴 Decide the minimum age — realistically 18

DPDP requires verifiable parental consent before processing a child’s personal data, where a child is anyone under 18. It also prohibits certain things outright for children, including behavioural tracking and targeted advertising.

“Verifiable” is the hard word. It does not mean a checkbox saying “my parent said yes”. Building an actual verifiable-parental-consent flow is a product surface of its own — identifying a parent, verifying that they are one, recording the consent, and handling its withdrawal.

So the realistic decision for a two-person company is 18+, stated at signup and in the terms. That is not a cop-out; it is choosing not to build a system you cannot build well, in an area where doing it badly is worse than not offering it.

Decided 2026-08-27 — 18+, and no under-18 flow

Token is 18 and over. Stated on the signup screen and in the terms, with both store age ratings aligned to it.

Cost: everyone under 18 is excluded, and that is a genuine market decision rather than a formality — a teenager selling something on a marketplace has exactly the problem this product solves.

Cost of the option rejected: supporting under-18s means building verifiable parental consent properly — identifying a parent, verifying they are one, recording the consent, handling its withdrawal. Weeks of work, in an area where a half-built version is worse than none, and it carries a hard prohibition on behavioural tracking that you would then have to prove you honour.

Two consequences to carry forward:

  • The tracking prohibition is already satisfied, by a decision taken for an unrelated reason. Analytics are self-hosted event counts with no per-user timelines (lesson 0007), so there is nothing behavioural to prohibit. Do not let a future growth experiment quietly undo that — it would reopen this.
  • Stating an age is not verifying one, and you must not start profiling users to guess. The policy below, for an underage account you discover, is the whole of your obligation and it is deliberately reactive.

The policy for an underage account you discover

1. What counts as discovering it: a report, something the
   user tells support, or an obvious signal. You are not
   required to hunt, and you must not start profiling users
   to guess ages.

2. What we do: suspend the account, tell the user why, and
   give a route to contest it if we are wrong.

3. What we delete, and when. Follow the erasure procedure
   from lesson 0001, including the backup tail.

4. What we tell the other party. Anyone holding a token
   issued by that account loses access. They are told the
   token is no longer active -- and nothing about why.

5. Where it is recorded, and who decided.

Point 4 is the Token-specific wrinkle. Suspending an account has consequences for a different person — the holder — who has done nothing wrong and is not entitled to know anything about the account holder.

2 · Consumer protection

Most of this activates when the paid tier ships. Write it now anyway: it is a stable document, it takes an afternoon, and it stops the paid tier being blocked on paperwork later. Lesson 0016 has the rest of the payments picture.

RequirementWhat it means in practice
Published refund and cancellation policyAt a stable URL, alongside the privacy policy and terms. Required once you charge
Auto-renewal disclosed clearly before purchaseThe price, the period, and that it renews — on the purchase screen, not in the terms
Renewal remindersBefore the charge, not after
Cancellation as easy as signupSame number of steps, same place, no retention maze
A published grievance contactCan be the same person and address as the DPDP officer
Acknowledge the appeal routeUsers can appeal your grievance decisions to the Grievance Appellate Committee. Say so
Consolidate the officer roles — one person, one address

You will end up with three named contacts if you are not careful: a DPDP grievance officer, an IT Rules grievance officer, and a consumer grievance contact. These can be one person with one published address, and consolidating them is the sensible default for a two-person company.

The reason is not laziness. Three published addresses is three things to keep accurate in four places each, and the failure mode is a stale address in one place that quietly turns a statutory contact into a dead letter. One address, published everywhere, changed in one place.

The cancellation flow, written out

ACCEPTABLE

Settings -> Subscription -> Cancel subscription
   "Your subscription ends on 12 March. You keep everything
    until then. After that your tokens stay live but you go
    back to the free limits."
   [ Cancel subscription ]   [ Keep it ]

One screen. States what happens. Both buttons are plain.


NOT ACCEPTABLE

  - A cancel button three levels deeper than subscribe
  - "Are you sure? You will lose ..." repeated twice
  - An offer that must be declined before cancelling
  - "No thanks, I do not care about my privacy"
  - Cancellation only by writing to support
  - A cancel button in grey and a keep button in blue

3 · Dark patterns

Read the CCPA Dark Patterns Guidelines and audit four flows against them: onboarding, consent, paywall, cancellation. The named patterns to check for:

PatternWhat it looks likeWhere Token could slip
Confirm-shamingA decline option phrased to make you feel bad“No thanks, I will keep giving out my number” on the paywall. Tempting, and exactly the thing
Forced actionRequiring something unrelated to proceedRequiring notification permission before the first token can be created
SneakingAdding something the user did not chooseA trial that converts silently; a default that was not shown
Interface interferenceVisually pushing one choice over anotherThe consent screen: if Allow is a button and Not now is grey text, consent was not free
Bait and switchAn action produces a different outcome than advertisedA “pause” that quietly expires the token
Drip pricingCosts revealed lateA per-token charge appearing after the subscription screen
Subscription trapEasy in, hard outThe flow above
NaggingRepeated prompts for something already declinedAsking for notification permission every launch
False urgencyCountdowns and scarcity that are not realNothing about Token is scarce. Do not invent it
“Pause instead” is evidence of good faith — keep it prominent

Most products hide the reversible, lower-commitment option because it converts worse. Token does the opposite: when a user goes to revoke, pause is offered first, because pause is reversible and revoke is not, and someone acting in anger should be offered the door they can walk back through.

That is the structural opposite of a dark pattern — the interface nudging toward the less committed choice, against your own interest in engagement. Keep it prominent, and note it in your dark-pattern audit as a deliberate design decision. It is a good answer to have on record.

One caveat, so this does not become the very thing it opposes: pause must not obstruct revoke. If “pause instead” adds a step someone has to decline before they can revoke, it has become an offer-you-must-refuse, which is on the list above. Both options, one screen, equally weighted.

Re-audit after any pricing or flow change 🔁

Dark patterns are almost never designed. They accumulate: a growth experiment here, a colour change there, an extra confirmation added after a support complaint. Each is defensible alone and the aggregate is a maze. Put the four-flow audit on the compliance calendar and re-run it after any pricing or onboarding change.

What this costs you

  • Everyone under 18, excluded. A real market decision, taken because verifiable parental consent is a product you would have to build properly or not at all.
  • Conversion. An honest consent screen, an easy cancellation and no confirm-shaming all convert worse than the manipulative versions. For this product that is the cost of being able to make the claim at all.
  • Behavioural analytics, given up entirely. Which you had already given up in lesson 0007 for a different reason, so this one is free.
  • A recurring audit that will keep finding small things, because dark patterns accumulate rather than being introduced.
