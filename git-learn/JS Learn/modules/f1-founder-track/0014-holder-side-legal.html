The Holder-Side Legal Surface

F1 — Lesson 14 of 16  |  the half of your userbase that never signed up

Why this matters for Token

Every conversation in Token has two people in it. One of them installed an app, read a consent notice, agreed to terms and has an account. The other one clicked a link.

That second person — the holder — is a user of your service. Their data is in your database, they are on your page, and they have exactly the same rights under DPDP as anyone who signed up. They are also, by design, the person you know least about and have the least ability to contact.

Most products in this shape simply forget them. That is a compliance gap and a support gap at the same time, and it sits on the page that is the entire recipient experience of the product.

1 · What you actually collect from a holder

Start here, because the notice is written from this list and nothing else. Take it from the RoPA in lesson 0001.

Two things that are not on that list, and must not get onto it
  • The holder’s network address from a call. Calls are relayed through your own server precisely so that neither party learns the other’s address. Writing those addresses into a call record defeats the policy from the far side, in a table nobody thinks of as sensitive. It is also a promise you make in the other direction — the user is protected from the holder learning theirs.
  • The token code, in any log. The code is a capability. It belongs in the request body and nowhere else — not in a URL path where it reaches access logs and browser history, not in application logs, not attached to an error report. Lesson 0009 covers the error tracker, which attaches request bodies by default and is the way this happens accidentally.

2 · The holder privacy notice

Short, plain, and written for someone who has no idea what Token is and did not choose to be here. A worked draft:

About this page

Someone gave you a code so you could reach them without
giving you their phone number. This page is how you reach
them.

What we collect from you
  - the name you typed, which we show to the person you
    are contacting
  - your messages, stored scrambled -- we cannot read them
  - your IP address and basic browser information, to keep
    the service working and to stop abuse
  - a record that you used this code, and when

What we do NOT collect
  - your phone number
  - your email
  - your location
  - anything about you from anywhere else

What we store in your browser
  A small amount of data so that refreshing the page does
  not throw you out of the conversation. Nothing for
  advertising. Nothing shared with anyone.

How long we keep it
  Your messages and the record of this conversation last as
  long as the conversation does. Your IP address and browser
  details are kept for a few days.

Your rights
  You can ask us what we hold about you, ask us to correct
  it, or ask us to delete it -- even though you do not have
  an account. Write to privacy@<domain>.

Who to contact
  Anything at all, including if you think this page is not
  really us: support@<domain>

By using this page you agree to our terms of use.  ->
Full privacy policy  ->
                                          Also in Hindi  ->

Five deliberate choices in that draft:

  • It opens by explaining the situation, not by naming the company. The holder does not know why they are here.
  • “What we do NOT collect” is a section. For a stranger landing on an unfamiliar page, the absences are more reassuring than the presences, and they are all true.
  • The storage line is there rather than a cookie banner. Say what is stored and why, once, in the notice.
  • Rights are stated explicitly, because a person with no account will assume they have none.
  • “Including if you think this page is not really us.” A cloned redemption page is the predictable attack on this product. Giving a suspicious holder somewhere to ask is both a safety measure and an early-warning signal for you — lesson 0015.

Linking it without ruining the page

The redemption page has one job and about four seconds to do it. A wall of legal text at the top defeats the product.

  • Footer link, small, always present. Not a modal, not an interstitial.
  • One line above the Connect button, at most: “By connecting you agree to the terms and privacy notice.” That is where agreement is given, so that is where it is disclosed.
  • Nothing that blocks the page. A holder who bounces off a consent wall is a failed redemption, and the redemption is your activation metric.

3 · Do holders accept terms by using the page?

Decide this explicitly and state it. There is no signup, so there is no signup checkbox, and the mechanism has to be something else.

OptionWhat it buysWhat it costs
Browsewrap — a footer link, use implies acceptanceZero frictionThe weakest form of agreement. Hard to argue someone accepted terms they never saw
Notice at the point of action — a line above Connect, plus the linkGenuinely disclosed at the moment of agreement, still almost frictionlessOne line of text on the page
Clickwrap — a checkbox they must tickThe strongest evidence of agreementFriction on the highest-value screen in the product, for a stranger with no investment
Decided 2026-08-27 — notice at the point of action

One line immediately above the Connect button — “by connecting you agree to the terms and privacy notice” — with both words linked, plus a persistent footer link. No checkbox, no modal, nothing that blocks the page.

The reason is that the holder is being bound to conduct rules that actually matter — no harassment, no abuse — and agreement is disclosed at the exact moment it is given, which a footer link alone cannot claim.

Cost of the option rejected: a checkbox is stronger evidence, and if you ever have to enforce the terms against a holder in a serious case you will wish you had one. That is a deliberate trade against redemption completion — which is your activation metric, on a screen where the visitor is a stranger with no investment and every reason to close the tab.

Also rejected: browsewrap, a footer link alone. Zero friction and the weakest possible footing for the one thing you might need to enforce.

What would reverse it: an actual enforcement attempt that turns on whether the holder agreed. Until that happens this is a theoretical weakness and the friction is a measurable one.

The wording is load-bearing and belongs on the QA checklist. If the line drifts below the button, becomes a greyed-out aside, or gets dropped in a redesign of the highest-value screen in the product, you have silently downgraded to browsewrap and nothing will warn you.

4 · A contact path that does not require an account

Everything in lesson 0008 about holders applies here, and this is where the link lives. What a holder may need:

  • Report the account holder. Abuse runs in both directions and the person on the web page has no app menu to fall back on.
  • Exercise their own data rights.
  • Ask whether a page is genuine.
  • Get help when the code is refused and they do not know why — which, remember, gives the same message whether the code is revoked, expired, used up or never existed. That is deliberate, and it means the holder genuinely cannot tell, so the contact path is their only route.

Whatever address you publish must be answerable by someone who cannot look the person up, because there is nothing to look up. Build the support process for that case explicitly.

5 · Hindi

Translate the notice alongside the page itself, in the same pass. A holder reading the page in Hindi and hitting an English-only privacy notice has been given a notice in a language they did not choose, which is not much of a notice.

This page has an audience the app does not: people who never chose your product, in whatever language their phone is set to. It is the one screen where the language default matters most and where you have the least control over who arrives.

Deferred 2026-08-27 — and this page is where deferring it costs most

Hindi is deferred across the product (lesson 0001). The reasoning holds and the trade is accepted — but it is worth being precise about why this page is the worst place for it, so the reversal condition is watched here first.

Everywhere else, an English-only screen is seen by someone who chose to install your app, and self-selected on that basis. The redemption page is seen by someone who was handed a link by a third party and did not choose anything. A delivery driver, a shop, a buyer replying to a listing. They arrive in whatever language their phone is set to, and they are the half of the product with no onboarding and no second chance.

The concrete consequence: a holder who cannot read the page does not write to support, they close the tab — and the token issuer concludes the product does not work. That failure is invisible to you, because it looks like a redemption that never happened.

So instrument it rather than guess. Redemption pages opened versus messages sent is already one of the five metrics in lesson 0007. If holder completion is poor, language is the first hypothesis to test, and it is testable cheaply — translate this one page before translating the app.

The order this implies is worth writing down: when Hindi does arrive, it arrives here first, not last. The redemption page and the holder notice are the highest-value strings in the product to translate, and they are also the fewest.

What this costs you

  • A second set of legal documents to write and keep in step with the first set. Two audiences, two languages, four documents.
  • Support for people who are not customers, cannot be looked up, and have no account. That cost is a direct consequence of the product design and it does not go away.
  • Weaker enforcement against a holder, chosen deliberately in §3 to protect redemption completion. Reversible if the evidence ever changes.
  • A little space on a page that has none to spare. The redemption page is the highest-stakes screen in the product for someone who did not ask to be there.
