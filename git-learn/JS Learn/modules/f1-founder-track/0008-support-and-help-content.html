Support & Help Content

F1 — Lesson 8 of 16  |  a published support contact is a store requirement, not a nicety

Why this matters for Token

A published support contact is one of the four UGC requirements on both stores. Missing it is an automatic rejection, which is why this lesson exists at all rather than being deferred to “after launch”.

But there is a harder reason. Token has a set of rules that are deliberately unforgiving — revoke cannot be undone, nobody at the company can restore an account, nobody can read a message to tell you what it said. Every one of those will arrive in support as an angry message from someone who has just lost something. Whether that is a catastrophe or a two-line reply depends entirely on whether you wrote the article before it happened.

1 · The published contact 🔴

One address, reachable without an account, published in three places: in the app, on the website, and in both store listings. It must be an address a human reads.

Holders have no account, and they are users of your service

This is the part almost every product in this shape gets wrong. The person who redeemed a token is using your service — on your page, under your terms, with their data in your database — and they have no app, no login, and usually no idea who you are.

Things a holder legitimately needs to contact you about:

  • They are being harassed by the account holder. The abuse runs both ways
  • The page will not load, or the code is rejected and they do not know why
  • They want their own data deleted — the name they typed, their address records. They are a Data Principal too, and this is a statutory right
  • They think the page is a scam and want to check whether it is really you. Lesson 0015 — a cloned redemption page is the predictable attack on this product, and a holder who suspects one needs somewhere to ask

So: a contact link in the footer of the redemption page itself, going to an address that does not require an account, and a support process that can handle “I am not a user and I need something” without asking them to sign in.

Pick tooling with a shared inbox, a per-conversation state, and canned replies. Anything that routes support mail into a personal mailbox will fail the first time one founder is on a flight.

2 · Response templates

Write the twenty most likely questions and their answers before launch. The first five are easy to guess and the rest come from your twelve testers — which is one more reason the closed test is worth running properly.

Three that carry the most weight, written out:

"I revoked a token by mistake. Please put it back."

Hello,

I am sorry -- revoking cannot be undone, by anyone, including
us. When you revoke, the code stops working immediately and
permanently. There is no version of the token still sitting
somewhere that we could switch back on.

That is deliberate. If we could reverse a revocation, then so
could anyone who got into our systems, and "revoke" would stop
meaning anything.

What you can do: issue a new token and send the new code to
the same person. Their old conversation stays where it is;
they will just reach you through the new code from now on.

-- <name>, Token support


"I lost my phone / changed my number. Get my account back."

Hello,

Signing in with your phone number will bring back almost
everything: your tokens, their labels and rules, who redeemed
what, and your conversation list.

The one thing it will not bring back is the text of your old
messages. Those are encrypted with a key that only ever
existed on your phone -- we never had a copy, so there is
nothing here for us to restore.

If you saved your 12-word recovery phrase when you signed up,
enter it during setup and your old messages come back too.
If you did not, everything else returns and the old message
text is gone.

-- <name>, Token support


"Someone is harassing me through a token I issued."

Hello,

Two things you can do right now, in this order:

1. Revoke that token. Open it and press Revoke. They lose
   access immediately.
2. Block the person. This is separate from revoking, and it
   is the important one -- a block follows the person across
   every token you have now or issue later.

Then report it, from the conversation menu. That sends us the
message and the context so we can act on it. We respond to
reports within 24 hours.

If you feel you are in danger, please contact the police.
We can provide records of when contact happened if they ask
us formally.

-- <name>, Token support
What support must never ask a user for

Write this list, put it in the support tooling where an agent sees it, and never break it — because the moment your own staff ask for these, every phishing attempt that asks for the same thing becomes plausible.

  • The 12-word recovery phrase. Never, for any reason, in any circumstance. There is no legitimate support flow that needs it, and asking for it trains users to give it away
  • A one-time code. Same reasoning — that is the login
  • A token code. It is a capability. Ask for the label or the token id instead
  • Payment details, whenever a paid tier exists
  • Screenshots containing message content, unless the user offers them. Use the in-app report instead, which carries proof of authorship

Put the same list in the help content, phrased for users: “We will never ask you for your recovery phrase or a one-time code. Anyone who does is not us.”

3 · The seven help articles that are actually hard

Ordinary help content writes itself. These seven are the ones where the product does something surprising, and each needs the reasoning, not just the instruction.

a. Why revoke cannot be undone

Because a reversible revocation is not a revocation. If the company could switch a token back on, then a mistake, a compromised admin account or a court order could too — and the promise that you can end a relationship instantly and permanently would be conditional. Give the user the alternative in the same breath: issue a new token, send the new code.

Say what revoke does not do, decided 2026-08-27: it ends contact; it does not delete the conversation. The user keeps their record of what was said, and the holder loses access and keeps no copy on your servers. People assume revoke wipes everything, and the surprise cuts both ways — someone who wanted the history gone will ask why it is still there, and someone who revoked in anger during harassment will be relieved that their evidence survived. One sentence in this article prevents both support threads:

Revoking ends their access. It does not delete your
conversation -- your side stays exactly as it is, and they
lose the page. If you want the conversation gone too, that
is a separate thing.

b. Why nobody at Token can restore an account

Distinguish the two halves, because collapsing them causes most of the confusion:

Comes back from your phone number aloneNeeds the recovery phrase
Tokens, labels, rules, expiry, use limitsThe text of old messages
Redemption history and conversation list—
Display name and avatar—
Timestamps and call records—

The left column is nearly everything. Saying so up front turns “I lost my account” into “I lost my old messages”, which is a much smaller conversation — and it is the truth.

c. Pause versus revoke

PauseRevoke
EffectContact stops nowContact stops now
ReversibleYes — resume any timeNo. Ever
Use it whenYou want quiet for a while, or you are not sureYou are finished with this relationship
The holder seesThat contact is unavailable right nowThat the code no longer works

Lead with pause in the article and in the app. It is the reversible option and the honest default for someone who is upset and may not want a permanent decision. Keeping “pause instead” prominent is also the opposite of a dark pattern and good evidence of it — lesson 0013.

d. What the other person sees, and why they need no app

Walk through it from the holder’s side, with a screenshot: they open a link, type a name, and they are in a conversation. They see your display name, not your number. They install nothing. They cannot look you up. This article does double duty as sales material — it is the answer to “why would anyone bother?”

e. What happens when a token expires

Cover all three ways a token can stop working without you doing anything, because users read “expired” and assume only the first: the expiry date passed; the use limit was reached; the token was paused or revoked. Explain that expiry cannot be extended after the fact — issue a new one — and that an expired token’s conversation stays visible to you.

The holder’s side of this article says less, on purpose

A holder whose code stops working sees one message, and it is the same message whether the code was revoked, expired, used up, never existed, or belonged to an account that has since been deleted. That sameness is deliberate — a page that distinguished them would let anyone work out which codes are real by trying them.

So the holder-facing help content cannot explain which case they are in, because you are not telling them. What it can say is what to do: ask the person who gave you the code for a new one, and here is where to write if you think the page itself is wrong. Support cannot resolve it either without the issuer, and that is worth saying plainly rather than leaving an agent to improvise.

f. How to hand over a token in person

This is the product rule article and it is the most important one on this list. A token must never travel over a channel that already identifies you — if you send it over your own WhatsApp, the recipient already has your number and the token protected nothing.

The safe paths, in order:

  1. They ask first. A business publishes a request link, your app generates and delivers the token from inside the system. No channel of yours is involved at all
  2. QR code. Show it on your screen, they scan it. There is no channel
  3. Typed into their form. Where a site asks for a phone number, type the code instead. The channel is theirs
  4. Spoken or printed. Read it out, write it on a card, put it in a listing
And the specific warning that belongs in this article

Pasting a token link into a chat app leaks the code to that platform before the recipient sees anything. WhatsApp, Telegram, Slack and iMessage all fetch a pasted URL to build the little preview card. That fetch reaches the platform’s crawler and is logged there — and the crawler is a real client, so on a single-use token it can burn the token before your recipient ever opens it.

The leak is the request, not what comes back, so nothing you put on the page prevents it. Users need to be told this in plain words: “do not paste the link into a chat app. Show the QR code, or read the code out.”

g. What the dead-link count actually measures

Any counter the app shows must have an article explaining what it counts, because users invent a meaning for a number and then act on it. Say exactly what is counted, what is not, over what window, and — most usefully — what it does not tell you. A count of failed attempts on a dead token means someone tried; it does not mean who, and it does not mean they are still trying.

4 · Incident communication templates

Three, written in advance, for the days when writing well is hardest.

SERVICE DOWN

Token is not working right now. We know, and we are on it.

  What is broken:  <plain description>
  What still works: <e.g. existing tokens are unaffected>
  Next update:     <a time, and then keep it>

Nothing you need to do. Your tokens are not affected by this.


A BUG CAUSED HARM

On <date> a bug in Token caused <what happened>.

  Who it affected:   <how many, and how they can tell>
  What it meant:     <the actual consequence, plainly>
  What we have done: <fix, and when it landed>
  What we owe you:   <if anything>

We are sorry. <One sentence on what changes so it does not
happen again.>


DATA BREACH

  -> Do not write this one here. It is in the incident
     runbook, lesson 0001, because it has legal notification
     clocks attached and a required format. Use that.

Two rules for all three: give a next-update time and keep it, even if the update is “still working on it”; and never speculate on cause while it is unresolved. A wrong cause published early is remembered longer than the outage.

What this costs you

  • A daily inbox from launch day, with a 24-hour standard you have published on two stores.
  • Support for people who are not your users — holders with no account, no record, and no patience. That is a real ongoing cost and it comes directly from the product design.
  • Angry conversations that you cannot fix, because the answer is genuinely “that is gone and nobody can bring it back”. Good articles make these shorter, not pleasant.
  • Writing time now — about a week for twenty templates and seven articles — against a much larger cost later of answering the same thing from scratch every time, differently.
