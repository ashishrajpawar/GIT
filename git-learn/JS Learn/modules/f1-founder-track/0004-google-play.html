Google Play

F1 — Lesson 4 of 16  |  ⌛ the longest pure-calendar item in the project

Start this today, whatever else is unfinished

A new personal Play developer account must run a closed test with at least 12 testers opted in for 14 continuous days before it can apply for production access. Fourteen days is calendar time. It cannot be shortened by working harder, and a tester dropping out can reset the clock.

Add account creation, identity verification, recruiting twelve real people, and the build actually being testable, and the honest lead time is 3–5 weeks minimum. That is the item most likely to be the last thing standing between a finished app and a launch date.

And note what it depends on: the closed test needs testers to be able to log in, and login is an SMS OTP, and OTPs do not deliver in India without DLT registration — which is itself 1–3 weeks. Read lesson 0012 today as well. Those two chains are the critical path of the whole launch.

Recruiting twelve testers is a real task, not an errand

They must opt in with a Google account, install from the closed track, and stay. People agree readily and then never install. Budget for that:

  • Ask sixteen to get twelve. Assume attrition rather than discovering it.
  • Send one message with the exact steps — the opt-in link, that they must be signed in as the account they gave you, and how to find the app afterwards. Most failures are the wrong Google account.
  • Check the console count once a week and chase immediately. A gap you notice on day thirteen is expensive.
  • Give them something to test. Twelve people staring at a login screen is not a test. Lesson 0010 has the checklist; hand it to them.
  • Get a light tester agreement signed first — confidentiality, no public screenshots, permission to use their feedback. It is one page. See lesson 0015.

And there is a Token-specific problem: the app needs two parties. A tester alone can issue a token and then has nobody to redeem it. Pair them, or give each tester a second browser tab pointed at the redemption page, and say so in the instructions.

Then the three declarations underneath, which are the ones reviewers verify:

  • Encrypted in transit — yes, and it must actually be true for every endpoint including the redemption web page.
  • Users can request deletion — yes, and this is checked against your deletion page, see §3.
  • Independent security review — only claim it if one happened.
Cross-check every answer against real app behaviour

The most common cause of rejection for a communications app is a Data Safety declaration that does not match what the app does — and the most common cause of that is a founder answering from the product vision instead of from the code.

Two Token-specific traps:

  • “We do not collect phone numbers.” You do. You store a scrambled form of one. Declaring “not collected” is a false declaration, and it is the single easiest thing for a reviewer to disprove — the sign-up screen asks for a phone number.
  • “Messages are not collected because they are encrypted.” They are collected; you store them. Encryption is a separate answer on the same form. Declare collection and the encryption, which is the strong position anyway.

Sit with the technical founder and answer the form from the schema. Then keep a dated copy of what you declared, so the next release can be checked against it.

Content rating — the IARC questionnaire 🔴

Answer it honestly, particularly the question about whether users can communicate with people they do not know. For Token the answer is yes: that is the product. Anyone with a token can reach the user, and the user may never have met them.

A higher age rating is a much smaller problem than a rating obtained by understating what the app does. Ratings acquired on inaccurate answers get revoked, and revocation is a suspension, not a correction.

Also make sure the rating you end up with agrees with the minimum age in your terms — lesson 0013. Two numbers, one story.

App access instructions for reviewers

A reviewer cannot test Token alone: half the product is a second person redeeming a token. Assume the reviewer has one device, no second party, and about four minutes of patience.

That last paragraph is not padding. Reviewers are specifically checking for reporting, blocking and deletion, and telling them exactly where those live converts a possible rejection into a two-minute check.

The rest of the forms

  • Target API level — Google raises the minimum every year and enforces it on new submissions before existing apps. Confirm the current requirement with the technical founder rather than assuming last year’s.
  • Ads declaration — none. Say so; it is a positive for a privacy product and an inconsistency if the SDK list suggests otherwise.
  • Permissions declaration — justify every permission the app requests. Camera for QR scanning, microphone for calls, notifications. If a permission is in the manifest and you cannot say what it is for in one sentence, it should not be there. Restricted permissions need a separate declaration form and a strong reason; a product like Token should need none of them.

3 · Policy requirements

🔴 UGC compliance — all four, or automatic rejection

Google requires four things of any app where users generate content that other users see. Missing one is a rejection, on both stores.

RequirementWhat Token providesWhere the evidence is
Content filteringServer-side scanning is impossible — messages are ciphertext. What exists instead: rate limits, code-enumeration defences, and user-configured rules that gate contact before it startsExplain this in the review notes. It is a design fact, not a gap
In-app reportingReport, on a conversation and on a message, from both the app and the redemption pageLesson 0006
Blocking abusive usersBlock that targets the person, across all your tokens — not just the current oneLesson 0006, and the test script that proves it
Published contactA support address reachable without an accountLesson 0008
The filtering answer needs writing carefully

“We cannot filter content because it is end-to-end encrypted” is true and, on its own, reads as a refusal to comply. The version that works explains what you do instead, and why it is not weaker:

“Message contents are end-to-end encrypted, so no server-side filter is possible. Abuse is handled by the recipient, who can read the message: they report it from inside the app, and the report carries the content and the context needed to act, with proof of who sent it. Reports are triaged within 24 hours. Contact is also gated before it begins — nobody can message a user without a token that user issued, and the user can pause or revoke it instantly.”

That last sentence is worth more than it looks. Most UGC apps let anyone contact anyone and then filter afterwards. Token denies contact by default. Say so.

🔴 The web-accessible account deletion page

Google requires a URL a person can visit without installing the app, that lets them request deletion of their account and data. In-app deletion alone does not satisfy this.

https://<domain>/delete-account

Delete your Token account

Enter your phone number. We will send a one-time code to
confirm it is you, then delete the account.

  [ phone number      ]   [ Send code ]

What gets deleted
  - your account and display name
  - every token you issued, and every rule on them
  - your conversations and their messages
  - your devices and notification settings

What happens to the other person
  Anyone holding one of your tokens loses access
  immediately. They keep no copy on our servers.

What is kept, and for how long
  Copies in our encrypted backups are overwritten within
  7 days. Records we are required by law to keep, such as
  a log of a legal request, are kept for as long as the
  law requires and no longer.

Cannot access your number any more? Write to
support@<domain> and we will verify you another way.

Three things that page must get right:

  • It states what is deleted and what is retained. A page that just says “your data will be deleted” is both a weaker compliance answer and a false one, because of the backup tail.
  • The retention sentence must match the retention policy and the privacy policy word for word. Three documents, one number. This is where they drift.
  • There is a route for someone who lost the number. Otherwise your deletion mechanism fails exactly the people most likely to need it — and a SIM change is common.

The screenshot problem, specific to Token

Screenshots of a messaging app all look identical, and yours will read as “another chat app” — which is the exact misreading you are already fighting. The screenshots have to carry the premise, because the person looking at them has not read a word of your description.

A sequence that tells the story in five frames:

  1. A form asking for a phone number, with a token being typed in instead. Caption: give this instead of your number.
  2. The token list, several tokens with meaningful labels and different states. Caption: one for each place. Revoke any of them.
  3. The rules screen. Caption: they can reach you Tuesday afternoon, twice, by text.
  4. The holder’s browser view. Caption: they need no app, and never see your number.
  5. A revoked token, with the page refusing. Caption: revoke, and it is over.

Frame 1 is doing the heavy lifting. It is the only one that answers “why would I want this?” before the user knows what a token is.

5 · Release

  1. Run the closed test for the full 14 days, watching the tester count.
  2. Collect and act on feedback. Google expects to see that the test was real; more importantly, twelve people using an unfamiliar product will find your comprehension problems for free.
  3. Apply for production access.
  4. 🔁 Monitor policy change announcements. Play policy changes are announced with a deadline and enforced on it.

The rejection playbook — write it before you submit

Likely reasonPrepared response
Data Safety does not match observed behaviourYour dated declaration plus the RoPA. Fix the declaration if they are right; show the mapping if they are not
UGC policy — cannot find reporting or blockingThe review notes with the exact paths, plus a short screen recording
Account deletion not reachable without the appThe deletion page URL, in the listing and in the notes
Reviewer could not test the core flowThe two-party instructions in §2, and a demo token that never expires
Permissions not justifiedThe one-sentence justification per permission, prepared in advance
Content rating inconsistent with the appRe-run IARC honestly. Do not argue this one
Privacy policy URL unreachable or genericA live URL about this app specifically

Rejections are normal and are usually a paragraph long. The playbook exists so that the response takes an hour rather than a week, because each round trip costs days.

What this costs you

  • 3–5 weeks of calendar you do not control, and it sits on the critical path behind DLT registration.
  • Social capital. Twelve people doing you a favour for a fortnight, whom you must actually keep engaged.
  • A registration fee, plus the time cost of organisation verification.
  • At least one rejection. Assume it. The playbook is what keeps it to days rather than weeks.
  • Permanent policy-watching. Play policies change on Google’s schedule and enforcement is automatic.
