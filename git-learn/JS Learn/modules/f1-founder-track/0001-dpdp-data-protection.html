Data Protection under DPDP

F1 — Lesson 1 of 16  |  ends in a document, not a quiz

Why this matters for Token

Four separate deliverables are generated from one document you have not written yet: the privacy policy, Google Play’s Data Safety form, Apple’s privacy nutrition labels, and the retention policy. That document is the Record of Processing Activities — the RoPA — and it is a table of every personal data field Token touches.

Write it once and the four downstream jobs become transcription. Skip it and you will answer the same questions four times from memory, differently each time, and the stores will notice: a mismatch between your Data Safety declaration and your Apple labels is one of the most common rejection reasons for a communications app.

1 · The vocabulary, in one table

The DPDP Act (2023) uses words that mean something specific. You will meet them in the store forms and in every draft counsel sends back, so learn them once.

The sentence you must never write

“Token does not collect your phone number.” It is false, and it is false in a document a regulator reads.

Token stores phone_hash — a scrambled form of the number — because that is how a user gets back into their account on a new phone. A ten-digit Indian mobile has a known prefix and a small enough space that anyone with the hash function can try every number and match them all. A hash of a phone number is a lookup key, not an anonymisation.

The accurate sentence, and the one the policy and the lessons use, is: “we store a scrambled version of your phone number, used only to sign you in.”

Two different claims get collapsed into one line and only one of them is true. What a token holder learns is nothing — not the number, not an email, not an identity. That is the product promise and it is unaffected. What the company collects is a hashed number and a display name. When you see “no phone number” anywhere in your own copy, work out which of the two it means before you publish it.

Now the four columns that turn a list into a RoPA:

  • Which fields a vendor processes rather than you. The push token reaches Expo, Google and Apple. The OTP reaches your SMS aggregator. Those rows need a data processing agreement behind them — lesson 0009.
  • Which are encrypted at rest, and which are end-to-end encrypted. They are not the same thing and the difference is the whole product. Encrypted at rest means the disk is encrypted and you hold the key. End-to-end means you hold no key at all. Only message bodies are the second kind.
  • Which are derived rather than collected. phone_hash is derived from something you never store. Say so — it is a genuinely better answer than most apps can give, and it is exactly the kind of detail that makes a privacy policy credible.
  • → Cross-check every row against the actual database schema with the technical founder, sitting together, reading the migration files. This is the step people skip and it is the only step that catches the field nobody remembered.
The trap: a column that stores a fact twice

When you do the cross-check, look for the same fact appearing in two places. Token’s schema deliberately counts uses from the conversations table rather than keeping a counter column, and derives a token’s displayed state from a status column plus two timestamps that the database forces to agree.

Why it matters to you and not just to the engineer: a fact stored twice has to be deleted twice, and an erasure request that clears one copy and misses the other is a breach of a promise you published. Every duplicate you find in the schema is a row you must track in the retention policy.

3 · Consent and notice

The consent notice is the screen a user sees at onboarding. Under DPDP it has to be itemised, in plain language, and not bundled — a person must be able to decline one thing without declining the service.

Here is a worked draft. Notice that each item names a purpose, and that two of the four are genuinely optional.

Before you start, here is exactly what we keep.

  1. Your phone number, scrambled.
     Why: it is how you sign in, and how you get your account back
     on a new phone. We cannot read it back into a number.
     Required. Without this there is no account.

  2. The name you choose to display.
     Why: people who hold your tokens see it, so they know who
     they are talking to. Use anything you like.
     Required.

  3. Notifications.
     Why: to wake the app when someone you gave a token to messages
     or calls you.
     Optional. The app works without it; you will see messages when
     you next open it.                                  [ Allow ] [ Not now ]

  4. Crash reports.
     Why: so we can fix the crash you just had.
     Optional.                                          [ Allow ] [ Not now ]

Your messages are end-to-end encrypted. We store them as
scrambled text and we hold no key, so nobody here can read them
even if asked.

Read the full privacy policy   ->
                                            [ Create my account ]

Four things that draft is doing on purpose:

  • Purpose sits next to the field, not in a separate policy. “Why” on the same line is what makes it a notice rather than a wall.
  • Required and optional are visibly different. If every item has the same button, consent was bundled no matter what the text says.
  • It states the limit of the encryption claim plainly. Not “nobody can ever intercept your messages” — which is a bigger claim than the design supports — but “we store scrambled text and hold no key”, which is exactly true.
  • The full policy is a link, not a checkbox. A checkbox saying “I have read the privacy policy” is a fiction and regulators treat it as one.

Consent versioning

Record which version of the policy each user accepted, and when. One row: user, policy version, timestamp, and which optional items they allowed. You will need it the first time someone asks what they agreed to, and you will need it every time you change the policy and have to work out who must be re-asked.

Withdrawal

Write down, before it happens, what breaks when consent is withdrawn:

WithdrawnWhat happensWhat the user is told
NotificationsPush token deleted; app still works“You will see messages when you open the app”
Crash reportsReporting disabled on that deviceNothing needed
Sign-in dataThis is account deletion. There is no partial versionThe full deletion flow — see §4

That third row is the honest one. Some consent cannot be withdrawn while the service continues, and saying so is better than pretending there is a toggle.

Hindi

Write the notice in English and Hindi. Not machine-translated and left alone — have a person read it back. A consent notice that is not understood is not consent, and this is the one screen where a clumsy translation is a legal problem rather than an aesthetic one.

4 · Rights handling

Four rights, four procedures. Each procedure is a page: who receives the request, how they check it is really that person, what they do, and by when.

RightWhat you must produceThe hard part
AccessA copy of their data in a usable formDeciding what is theirs — see the warning below
CorrectionFix or complete what is wrongAlmost nothing here is user-editable except the display name; say so
ErasureDelete it, including from backupsThe backup tail. It is not instant and you must not claim it is
NominationLet a user name someone to exercise their rights if they die or become incapacitatedDPDP requires the mechanism. Most products have not built it
The export trap: a row in your table is not the same set as the user’s personal data

Redemption records hold the holder’s IP address, the name they typed, and their user agent. Those rows belong to a token the user issued, so an export written straight from the schema will include them.

Do not. The holder is a separate Data Principal who never signed up to your service and never agreed to be disclosed to anyone. Shipping their IP address to the person who issued the token would hand one person another person’s rough location, through a compliance feature, on request.

What belongs in the issuer’s export is that a redemption happened, and when. Write the export from the question “whose data is this?”, never from the table list.

The export format

Decide what a user actually receives, and make it something a human can open. A worked shape:

token-export-2026-08-26.zip
  |- account.json          display name, when you joined, which
  |                        policy version you accepted
  |- tokens.json           every token: label, when issued, rules,
  |                        expiry, current state. NO codes -- see below
  |- conversations.json    who redeemed what and when, and the
  |                        holder name they typed
  |- messages.json         your messages, decrypted on your device
  |                        before the file is written
  |- README.txt            what each file is, in plain language

Two decisions embedded in that:

  • No token codes in the export. The code is a capability — anyone holding it can contact the user. It is returned exactly once, when the token is created, and the system stores only a hash and an encrypted copy. Putting every code a user ever issued into a downloadable zip would undo that in one file.
  • Messages are decrypted on the device, not by the server. The server genuinely cannot produce them. If the export is generated server-side, the messages file contains ciphertext and a line explaining why — which is a strange-looking export and a completely honest one.

Then test each procedure end to end on a dummy account. Not a walkthrough on paper. Create an account, issue tokens, redeem one, send messages, then request access, correction and erasure through the same channel a real user would use, and time it.

5 · The grievance officer

A named person, with a working email and a postal address, published in the app and on the website. This is statutory and it is one of the six things that stops launch.

Grievance Officer
<Full name>
<Company legal name>
<Street address>
<City, State, PIN>
grievance@<your-domain>

We acknowledge every grievance within 24 hours and aim to
resolve it within the timeline the law sets. If you are not
satisfied with our decision you may appeal to the Grievance
Appellate Committee.

Three practical notes:

  • One person can hold several roles. The DPDP grievance officer, the IT Rules grievance officer and the consumer grievance contact can be the same human with one published address. Consolidating them is normal and reduces the number of addresses you have to keep accurate. Lesson 0013 covers the consumer half.
  • The address has to be real. A person must be able to post a letter to it. If your company address is a co-working desk, that is still an address; a PO box that nobody empties is not.
  • 🔁 Log every grievance — who, when, what, what you decided, when you closed it. The log is the evidence that the office exists in practice and not just on a page.

6 · Retention and deletion

Every data class in the RoPA gets a retention period, and every period gets a written justification. That second half feels like busywork until someone asks why you keep holder IP addresses, and the answer “we never decided” is much worse than any number you could have picked.

For Token, short retention is a selling point, not a compliance cost. You are the product that exists so people can stop handing out permanent identifiers. Act like it.

Four questions the policy must answer explicitly, because they are the ones nobody thinks of until a user asks:

  1. What happens to message content when a token is revoked? Revocation stops future contact. Does it delete the conversation that already happened? Decide, then make the app say the same thing the policy says.
  2. What happens to a shared group when its owner deletes their account? Somebody else is in it. Deleting one person’s account cannot silently delete another person’s history.
  3. How does deletion propagate to backups? This is the one below.
  4. What does a revoked token leave behind? The row survives, because you need to be able to answer “why was I refused”. Say what the row contains.
Backup retention is a property of the data, not of one folder

Suppose your policy says: “copies in our encrypted backups are overwritten within 7 days.” That is a claim about every copy.

A backup script that prunes old dumps on the server satisfies it there. But the copy that goes off-site, and the copy on a second machine, are usually made with commands that never delete anything at the destination — that is precisely why those commands feel safe to run. The property that makes them safe is the property that makes your published sentence false, and nothing errors either way.

So: every location a backup reaches needs the same clock. The server, the off-site copy, the bucket’s own versioning and lifecycle rules, and any backups your hosting control panel takes on its own. Ask the technical founder to list the locations, and count them yourself.

Also mind the arithmetic. “Delete anything older than 7 days” usually means more than 7 whole days, and a daily backup adds up to another 24 hours on top. If you advertise seven days, the mechanism must run at six.

And keep a floor under it. Age-based deletion with nothing beneath it destroys every copy you own at exactly the moment your backups have been failing quietly for a fortnight. Keep the most recent verified backups whatever their age — and report it when that floor holds something past the published window, rounding the excess up. A breach of half a day rounded down to zero reads as compliance. Silently deleting your last good backup is a data-loss incident you chose; silently keeping it is a lie. Both costs are real; pick one on purpose and write down which.

7 · Breach response

Write the incident response runbook now, while nothing is on fire, because the entire value of a runbook is that it was written by someone who was calm.

1. DETECT      Who notices? Alerts, a user report, a researcher email,
               a vendor telling you. All four are real routes.

2. ASSESS      What data, how many people, is it still happening?
               Severity tier decided here. Named decider, plus a
               named deputy for when they are on a flight.

3. CONTAIN     Stop the bleeding. Rotate the credential, take the
               endpoint down, revoke the token. Containment beats
               diagnosis -- you can investigate a stopped incident.

4. NOTIFY      Two different clocks, two different recipients:
                 - the Data Protection Board, on the DPDP timeline
                 - CERT-In, on a much shorter one (lesson 0012)
                 - affected users, in plain language
               Do not merge these into one process. Different
               thresholds, different formats, different deadlines.

5. LEARN       Write what happened and what changes. Publish what
               you can. Update this runbook.

Severity tiers — a worked example

TierLooks likeWho decidesClock
S1Personal data exposed to someone who should not have it, or is still being exposedEither founder, immediately, aloneNotification clocks start now
S2Access to internal systems, no confirmed data exposureBoth foundersAssess within hours; may still be reportable
S3Bug with privacy implications, no exposure — a wrong badge, a leaked identifier in a logTechnical founderFix in the next release; log it

Note that S3 is a real tier and not padding. A token code written into a log file is exactly that: nobody outside got it, and it is still a capability sitting in a place nobody designed for capabilities.

The user notification template

Subject: Something went wrong, and here is what it means for you

On <date> we found that <plain description>.

What was affected:      <the specific data>
What was NOT affected:  <especially: your message contents, which
                        we store scrambled and cannot read>
What we have done:      <the fix, and when it landed>
What you should do:     <concrete, or "nothing" if that is true>

We are required to report this to the authorities and we have.
If you have questions, write to grievance@<domain>.

-- <name>, <company>

The “what was NOT affected” line is not spin. For a product whose whole claim is that the server holds no key, the most important fact in a breach notice is usually the thing that could not have leaked — and you can only say it if it is true.

Then run one tabletop exercise. Pick a plausible scenario — a leaked database credential, say — sit down for an hour with the technical founder, and walk the five steps out loud. You will find at least two things missing. Everyone does. Do it again quarterly. 🔁

8 · The policy documents

  • ⚖️ Brief counsel with the RoPA attached. That single attachment is what turns a generic template into a policy about your product. Send it with a one-page cover note: what Token is, what a token is, what a holder is, and the three facts that make it unusual — a hashed phone number instead of an email, end-to-end encrypted messages the server cannot read, and holders who use the service without accounts.
  • Verify the policy against actual behaviour, not intended behaviour. Read it line by line next to the app. If it says you delete within 30 days, find the code that does it. This is the same check as the RoPA cross-check and it catches different things.
  • Publish at a stable public URL before store submission. Both stores want the link in the listing, and the link must work before you submit, not after.
  • 🔁 Review it after every feature that touches data. Add it to the release checklist in lesson 0010 so it is a step someone ticks rather than a good intention.

What this costs you

  • Two to three weeks of your own time, most of it on the RoPA and the cross-check, and it is genuinely tedious. Everything downstream is faster for it.
  • Counsel fees for the policy and terms, plus 2–4 weeks of turnaround you do not control. Start the brief before you think you are ready.
  • A standing operational load you did not have before: a grievance mailbox that must be answered, a log that must be kept, a runbook that must be rehearsed. It is small, and it is never zero again.
  • Some product friction on purpose. An unbundled consent screen converts slightly worse than a single “Agree and continue” button. That is the trade, and for this product it is also the marketing.
