Decisions That Survive

C0 — Lesson 2 of 2  |  ~45 min

Why this matters for Token

You will decide, this year, that every call goes through your own relay server even when two people are in the same room. Someone — possibly you, in March, looking at a bandwidth bill — will want to turn that off.

Whether that is a five-minute conversation or a three-week re-argument depends entirely on one thing: whether the original decision was written down with its cost. Not whether it was written down. Whether the cost was.

That is the whole lesson, and the exercise is the function that answers “which decision governs this question now?” when some decisions have replaced others.

1 · The failure this prevents

Here is a real sequence from this project, compressed.

March    "Relay every call through our own server, so neither
          party learns the other's IP."
          Written in a chat message. Everyone agrees.

June     A new lesson is written about call setup. The author
          knows relay-only is the rule, and also knows direct
          connections have better latency. Reasonably, they
          make it a user setting -- normal ICE by default for
          call quality, relay-only for anyone who wants it.

          Nobody notices. It is not obviously wrong. The
          original reasoning is in a chat message nobody can
          find, so there is nothing to check it against.

August   Someone asks why the capacity plan assumes 15% of
          calls use the relay. It assumes that because the
          setting defaults to off.

          The privacy property the product is built on has
          been optional for five months.

The June author did nothing unreasonable. They made a sensible trade-off between quality and privacy, which is exactly what you want engineers to do — unless that trade-off has already been made, deliberately, for a reason they cannot see.

The reason a chat message is not a decision record

Not because it is informal. Because it is not where anyone will look, and because it almost never contains the part that matters.

“Relay every call” is a conclusion. The June author needed the argument: that an ICE candidate is an IP address; that a token holder is often a delivery company; that a direct path therefore hands a stranger the issuer's home address; and that the person who bears the cost is not the person who chooses, so it cannot be a user setting.

Given that paragraph, nobody writes the June lesson. Given the conclusion alone, writing it is the sensible thing to do.

2 · What an ADR is

An architecture decision record. One file per decision, numbered, in the repo, next to the code it governs. Token's live in token/docs/adr/.

The shape is fixed and it is short:

# ADR-0008 -- Relay-only ICE on every call

- Status: accepted
- Date:   2026-08-20

## Context
  What was true, what problem appeared, and -- critically --
  what the options were. An ICE candidate is an IP address.
  A holder is often a delivery company. Etc.

## Decision
  What we are doing. One paragraph, unambiguous.

## Consequences
  What this costs. Every call is relayed, so relay bandwidth
  is a real line item and a coturn outage is a TOTAL call
  outage rather than a degraded one.

## Alternatives rejected
  What we did not do, and why. This is the section people
  skip and the section that does the work.
Three rules that make the difference
  1. Written when the decision is made, not afterwards. A record written three months later contains what you remember, which is the conclusion. The argument is the part that fades.
  2. The cost goes in. A decision recorded with its cost can be overturned when the evidence changes. One recorded without can only be re-argued from nothing, because a future reader cannot tell whether the trade-off was considered and accepted or simply never noticed.
  3. Append-only. A superseded record is marked, never deleted. This is the one people fight, and it is the subject of the rest of this lesson.

3 · Supersession, and why deleting is worse than it looks

Decisions change. The obvious thing to do with a record that is no longer true is delete it, or edit it in place. Both destroy the thing you were keeping.

Consider what a reader in six months actually needs. They are looking at a piece of code that seems wrong. The question in their head is not “what is the rule?” — they can read the rule. It is “was this considered?”

If you...The reader six months later seesWhat they do
Delete the old recordOnly the current ruleRe-argues the rejected option from scratch, having no way to know it was rejected
Edit it in placeA rule that appears always to have been this wayCannot tell which parts were reasoned and which were retrofitted
Mark it superseded and add a new oneBoth, and the reason for the changeReads two paragraphs and moves on

So the records accumulate, and answering “what governs this now?” becomes a small computation. That is the exercise.

The chain

“The highest number wins” is the answer everyone reaches for, and it is wrong for a reason worth seeing rather than being told.

The rule is not about numbers at all. A record is in force when nothing supersedes it — that is the definition, and it is the only one that survives records being written out of order, renumbered, or imported from another project.

4 · The two cases that make this a function rather than a filter

A cycle

Two records can end up pointing at each other. It happens through ordinary mistakes: someone marks 0004 superseded by 0008, then later decides the change was wrong and marks 0008 superseded by 0004 instead of writing a new record.

An empty result is the wrong answer twice over: it is indistinguishable from “we never decided this”, and it hides a defect in the records themselves. A cycle must be refused explicitly, not resolved by guessing.

A dangling pointer

A record superseded by an id that does not exist. Usually a typo, occasionally a record that was deleted by someone who had not read rule 3.

This one is genuinely ambiguous, so the decision has to be deliberate. Two defensible answers:

  • Treat it as still in force — nothing that exists supersedes it. Optimistic, and it silently accepts a broken reference.
  • Refuse — the records are inconsistent and a human should look.

Token refuses. The reason is the one from lesson 1: a gap in the data and the data working are different states and need different responses. A dangling pointer is a gap. Silently treating it as “in force” means a decision could be resurrected by a typo.

One that is broken on purpose

This runs and returns something plausible. Work out which case it gets wrong.

It never returns. The loop follows 0004 to 0008 to 0004 forever, and the only thing that stops it is the playground's two-second budget. A cycle is not an edge case you can leave undefined — unhandled, it is a hang, and in a server it is a hang that takes a request thread with it.

5 · What this looks like when it works

Token has fifteen ADRs. Two of them exist only because a previous decision was recorded properly enough to be challenged:

RecordWhat it did
ADR-0008Made relay-only mandatory rather than a setting. It could be written quickly because the earlier realtime decision had recorded why the relay existed, so the argument was about one sentence rather than the whole design
ADR-0013Put a floor under the backup retention promise. It exists because the retention decision had written down its cost — and reading that cost is what surfaced the case where deletion destroys your last good backup

Both are examples of the same thing: the cost section is where the next decision comes from. A record that lists only benefits produces no successors, because there is nothing in it to notice.
