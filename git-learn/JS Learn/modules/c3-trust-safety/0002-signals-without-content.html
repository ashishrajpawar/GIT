The Signals You Have Without the Content

C3 — Lesson 2 of 3  |  ~50 min

Why this matters for Token

The previous lesson dealt with what somebody reports. This one deals with everything nobody reports, which is nearly all of it.

Token's server cannot read a single message, and it can still see a great deal: who sent to whom, when, how often, which token was used, how old the account is, and how many people have revoked on that sender. None of it is content and all of it is real.

The trap is treating those numbers as verdicts. Volume is not intent, and a system that suspends on a counter will suspend a small business answering forty customers on a Monday. Signals are for deciding what to look at. Reports are for deciding what to do.

1 · The envelope is not encrypted, and cannot be

A message row holds ciphertext the server cannot open. It also holds the things the server needs in order to deliver it at all:

id            881207
conversation  4471
sender        4102
created_at    2026-08-29T14:03:11Z
body          \x8f21a0...        <- the part nobody can read

You cannot encrypt the routing information, because the router needs it. That is not a weakness in the design; it is what routing is. So the honest description of Token's guarantee stays exactly as C5 states it: the server holds only ciphertext and cannot read messages. It does not say, and must never say, that the server knows nothing.

2 · The five signals, and what each one actually catches

SignalCatchesAlso catches
Messages per token per hourSpam burstsA support conversation going well
Distinct recipients per senderBroadcast farmingA shop that issued forty tokens on purpose
Token redemption velocityAutomated harvestingA QR code on a busy counter
Revocation rate against a senderUsers voting with revocationsSomebody whose tokens simply expired
Account age against send volumeThrowaway accountsAn enthusiastic new user

The third column is the whole lesson. Every one of these has a perfectly innocent population that looks identical from the outside, and no amount of tuning separates them, because the difference between harassment and enthusiasm is in the content and the content is exactly what you cannot see.

The base rate is what decides whether a threshold is useful

Suppose 10 senders out of 100,000 are abusive, and your detector is 99% accurate in both directions — far better than anything you will actually build.

It catches about 10 of the 10. It also flags 1% of the other 99,990, which is about 1,000 innocent people. Roughly 99% of your queue is wrong, and no reviewer survives that; they start clearing the queue rather than reading it, which is the failure mode from C2/0001 arriving in a different building.

This is why a signal ranks rather than decides. A 99%-wrong queue that is a priority order for human attention is useful. The same queue wired to an automatic suspension is a machine for punishing strangers.

3 · Counting is harder than it looks, in three specific ways

The counter must be shared

ADR-0003 says the API is stateless and runs behind N replicas. A counter held in a node's memory therefore counts a third of the traffic on a three-replica deployment, and a limit of 100 becomes a limit of 300 that nobody wrote down. Counters live in Redis, as shared state, for the same reason socket fan-out does.

The window boundary

A fixed window — “100 per hour, reset on the hour” — permits 200 in two minutes, by sending 100 at 10:59 and 100 at 11:01. Both hours are within their limit and the burst you were trying to prevent happened anyway.

Distinct means distinct

“Distinct recipients” and “number of messages” answer completely different questions. Forty messages to one courier is a conversation; one message to forty strangers is a broadcast. Counting the array length gets the second one right by accident and the first one catastrophically wrong.

4 · Metadata is not harmless, and the retention is the answer

Everything above is a behavioural record of real people. Token's own ADR-0012 says analytics are event counts with no per-user timelines — that is the mechanism which discharges the obligation not to profile, and it would be undone by an abuse system quietly building the timeline instead.

Two things keep both true at once, and they are worth stating as rules rather than as habits:

  • Abuse counters are short-lived by construction. Redis keys with a TTL matched to the window, not rows in a table. They answer “is this happening now” and then they are gone.
  • They are used for one purpose. Ordering a review queue. Not recommendations, not growth experiments, not a per-user profile that exists because it turned out to be easy.
The cost, stated rather than glossed

A window that expires means you cannot investigate a pattern from three months ago. When a report arrives about a slow, patient campaign, the counters that would have shown it have already gone, and all you have is what is inside the current window.

That is a genuine loss and it is the accepted price of not keeping a behavioural record of everybody in order to catch a few. The reversible version, if evidence ever demands it, is a longer window — not a permanent one.
